Global cotton remains in mild oversupply in 2025–26, with world lint production projected
around 26 million tonnes against consumption near 25.2–25.7 million tonnes, keeping stocks
historically high even as the gap narrows slightly. Prices are subdued versus the post‑pandemic
peak: the Cotlook A Index has traded mostly in the mid‑70s cents per pound range, and
international futures are hovering near the low‑60s cents per pound, though late‑2025 and
early‑2026 showed some firming from five‑year lows. Brazil and the United States remain the
dominant exporters, while Asian spinning hubs led by Bangladesh, Vietnam, China, India and
Pakistan anchor import demand, with India’s own imports picking up as domestic quality and
price conditions fluctuate.[ 2][ 4][ 6][ 8][ 10][^11]
India, China, Brazil, the United States, Pakistan and Australia together account for the bulk of
global production, with China (~35.5 million bales), India (23.5 million), Brazil (19.5 million), the
United States (13.9 million), Pakistan (5.0 million) and Australia (4.5 million) forming the core
supply base in 2025–26. Within India, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Telangana are now the top
producing states in the 2025–26 advance estimates, while Karnataka and Rajasthan form the
second tier. State‑level wholesale prices in December 2025 clustered around ₹7,000–7,800 per
quintal (roughly ₹70–78 per kg), with Telangana and Maharashtra at the higher end and Gujarat
and Rajasthan somewhat lower, reflecting quality, logistics and MSP procurement dynamics.[
13][ 14][ 9][^16]
Geopolitics and logistics remain key risk drivers: the Russia–Ukraine war has lifted energy and
chemical costs, the US–China trade conflict continues to reshape cotton and textile flows, and
the Gaza‑linked Red Sea crisis has lengthened routes and raised freight and insurance,
especially on Asia–Europe lanes. Climate risk is rising in major cotton belts, with half of global
cotton‑growing regions expected to face high exposure to at least one climate hazard (heat,
drought, flood, or storms) by 2040, and India, Pakistan, West Africa, northern China and parts
of Australia particularly exposed. For mills and textile groups, the next 2–3 years likely bring
modest demand recovery, continued pressure on margins from low yarn and fabric prices, and a
premium on supply‑chain resilience, diversified sourcing and risk‑managed raw‑cotton
procurement.[ 18][ 20][ 22][ 24][ 26][ 28][^29]

Policy and support systems. China maintains domestic cotton prices above world levels via
target price‑based subsidies in Xinjiang (roughly 18,600 yuan/ton or about 2,620 USD/ton),
keeping mill‑gate prices significantly above the A Index and US levels. India’s MSP, CCI
procurement and import duties (currently 11 percent on raw cotton) underpin a floor for
Shankar‑6 and other domestic benchmarks, which often trade at a small premium or discount to
the A Index depending on crop quality and currency. In contrast, Brazil and Australia are highly
export‑oriented, with prices strongly arbitraged to ICE futures and the A Index, minus logistics
and quality adjustments, leading to quotes only a few cents from global benchmarks.[ 6][
8][ 43][ 7][^9]
Quality and origin premiums. Australian and US cottons often earn premiums for staple length,
strength and contamination‑free status, supporting slightly higher merchant prices than
similarly situated origins, especially in high‑count yarn markets. Indian Shankar‑6 trades near
or slightly above the A Index in periods of tight domestic supply because of its suitability for a
wide range of ring‑spun yarns and strong local demand. Brazilian cotton has gained share by
offering consistent quality and competitive fob prices, but domestic oversupply in 2025 pushed
CEPEA’s domestic index down nearly 17 percent even as Brazil’s share of global trade reached
about 31 percent.[ 8][ 37][ 33][^7]
Currency and macro factors. Exchange‑rate swings significantly shape local‑currency prices: in
2025 the US dollar appreciated about 5 percent versus the Indian rupee (USD/INR average ≈87.2),
while depreciating roughly 12.5 percent against the Brazilian real (USD/BRL average ≈5.59),
altering relative export competitiveness. A stronger real amplified the fall in Brazil’s
dollar‑denominated prices, while rupee depreciation cushioned Indian farm‑gate prices in local
terms despite weaker global benchmarks.[ 48][ 6][ 9]

increase the cost of holding inventory and the risk of mismatches between raw‑cotton
purchase prices and yarn or fabric realisations.[ 19][^61]
Most medium‑term forecasts point to modest growth in global cotton consumption, with USDA
and ICAC expecting mill use to edge higher as macro conditions stabilise, though cotton’s share
of global fibre demand may remain under pressure from synthetics. ICAC’s longer‑term Outlook
suggests that by the mid‑2030s China and India will remain the top producers (each around 22–
23 percent of global output), with Brazil surpassing the US as the third‑largest producer,
reinforcing the Americas‑plus‑Asia supply axis. Market‑research firms such as Technavio project
the global cotton market to grow by roughly USD 9.6 billion between 2026 and 2030 at a CAGR
of about 3.4 percent, driven by population, income growth and continued demand for natural
fibres.[ 1][ 55]
